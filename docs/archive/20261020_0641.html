Abstract
A mixed signal vector composed of a weighted sum of random, unobserved endmember vectors has a mean and a variance that both depend on the unknown weights. This suggests that linear estimates of the weights based on the mean model are likely to be inefficient, and could possibly be improved upon by estimates that also make use of the variance model. In the context of a latent factor model for source variability, we calculate theoretically how much information is available in the variance model, and show how this information may stabilize inference in the case of an ill-conditioned mean model. We show through several data examples that utilization of the variance model is likely to improve parameter estimation in real-world scenarios.

An exact finite-state representation of viscoelastic memory for accelerated sea-level modeling: SLEvolve v1.0
Authors: Xiyuan Bao, S. Mostafa Mousavi, Konstantin Latychev, Jerry X. Mitrovica
First: 2026-10-16T17:05:23+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T17:05:23+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Glacial Isostatic Adjustment (GIA) models solve the gravitationally self-consistent sea-level equation for the response of the solid Earth and the oceans to changing ice loads. As these models are increasingly embedded in coupled ice-sheet simulations, glacial-cycle calculations, and large ensembles, standard pseudo-spectral 1-D solvers face a steep computational barrier: they evaluate the viscoelastic response by explicitly re-summing every past load increment, scaling quadratically with time steps as $O(N^2)$. Here we show that this computational penalty is an artifact of algorithmic formulation rather than Earth physics. By exploiting the exponential form of the viscoelastic relaxation kernel, we reformulate the viscous response as an exact recursive update of a finite set of modal states, without re-summing the load history. This exact reformulation alone reduces the cost of a run to $O(N)$, yielding a 20.6-fold speed-up relative to a standard full-history 1-D GIA solver for a case study involving a 550-step Antarctic future sea-level projection. Implemented in our open-source solver SLEvolve v1.0, this formulation---combined with fewer, table-free and multithreaded spherical-harmonic transforms, improved file handling, and physics-inspired iteration starts---achieves an end-to-end speed-up of over three orders of magnitude (up to 4,000-fold) against the same 1-D GIA solver, reducing day-long simulations to under thirty seconds on a workstation. Beyond this solid-Earth application, SLEvolve illustrates how human-derived analytical reformulations can be combined with benchmark-harnessed AI-assisted engineering to modernize scientific software. In particular, Numerical regression tests and performance profiling guided the AI agents' implementation choices and helped reject ineffective optimizations.

Neural Operators Reveal Scale-Dependent Limits on Recovering Greenland Ice Loss from Sea Level Fingerprints
Authors: Xiyuan Bao, Sophie Coulson, Natasha Valencic, S. Mostafa Mousavi, Sönke Dangendorf, Andrew Jason Lloyd, Jerry X. Mitrovica
First: 2026-10-16T16:55:02+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T16:55:02+00:00
Comments: 33 page ,11 figures
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Sea level fingerprints connect the spatial distribution of rapid ice mass change to sea surface height (SSH) change, but the spatial resolving power of this inverse problem remains poorly quantified. Here we provide the first systematic, scale-resolved quantification of Greenland Ice Sheet mass change structure recoverable from its SSH fingerprint when ice loss geometry varies. We develop a physics-informed neural operator, constrained by an accurate neural-operator emulator of the forward sea level equation, to probe the information content of this ill-conditioned inverse problem. Across synthetic ice change ensembles, broad ice loss patterns can be recovered, but distinct ice fields can produce nearly identical fingerprints, so fine-scale structure depends on the assumed a-priori ice change distribution. Inversion fidelity varies with the wavelength and amplitude of ice mass change. An empirical error law separates these effects and defines a scale-dependent accuracy ceiling. For a satellite-altimetry-derived reference field, the estimated fingerprint-only ceiling is $R^2\sim0.79$ under the present SSH observation geometry and matched-prior assumption. The leading three principal components explain 99.76% of fingerprint variance, yet higher-order components retain information needed to distinguish finer ice change structure. SSH observations near Greenland carry most information on ice loss locations. Applying the leading fingerprint principal components to the observed 1993--2019 SSH rate recovers the large-scale Greenland fingerprint, while the residual field is correlated with basin-scale bathymetry, consistent with unresolved sterodynamic variability. These results establish a quantitative empirical bound on fingerprint-only ice-loss resolving power and identify how independent observations and ice sheet constraints can improve inference.

Abstract
We use the angular distribution of top quark pairs ($t\bar{t}$) in the semileptonic final state $b\bar{b}\,\ellνq\bar{q}'$ to construct an anomaly detector for non-$t\bar{t}$ events. Wigner $D$ functions expand the distribution with coefficients determined by orthogonal series density estimation. Its equiprobable surfaces bin the events, the non-$t\bar{t}$ concentrate at low $t\bar{t}$ density. The performance is comparable to normalizing flows at parton level, but is limited by jet assignments at reconstruction level.

Remote Influences of Land Surface Temperature and their Implications for Sea Surface Temperature Patterns
Authors: Bosong Zhang, Timothy M. Merlis
First: 2026-03-30T14:16:47+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T15:33:14+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
The spatial pattern of sea surface temperature (SST) plays a central role in shaping the climate system, yet the influence of land surface temperature (LST) on large-scale SST patterns remains understudied. Using a state-of-the-art coupled climate model, we investigate the climate response to regional abrupt LST warming and time-dependent ramped-warming. We find that regional LST warming can substantially alter the mean-state SST pattern, with the response depending strongly on the location of land warming. In particular, warming over South America strengthens the zonal SST gradient across the tropical Pacific, producing a more La Niña-like mean state. Enhanced land warming increases the zonal contrast in diabatic heating, which excites stationary Rossby wave responses that strengthen alongshore winds and coastal upwelling in the eastern tropical Pacific, providing a dynamical pathway linking regional land warming to changes in the equatorial Pacific mean state. Similar remote SST responses occur for warming over North America, which is accompanied by cooling in the North Pacific, and over Central Africa, which is accompanied by cooling in the tropical Atlantic. In contrast, warming over the Maritime Continent and Tibetan Plateau produces little systematic change in large-scale SST patterns. These results demonstrate that regional LST changes can exert a substantial and geographically dependent influence on remote ocean temperatures. They further suggest that biases in both the mean state and warming trends of LST may contribute to biases in simulated SST patterns in historical simulations, highlighting the importance of accurately representing LST in climate models.

Abstract
Our recent research on Langevin dynamics with self-harmonic drift and classical spins has revealed a strong connection to ``Stochastic Localization," a concept attracting attention in the fields of mathematical geometry and data science. Distributional signatures of computation: inferring mechanisms from computation time distributions
Authors: Céline van Valkenhoef, Catherine Schuman, Nikolaus Kriegeskorte, Philip Walther
First: 2026-10-16T15:22:34+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T15:22:34+00:00
Comments: 12 pages, 4 figures, separate supplementary information
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Computational complexity theory provides a framework for analysing abstract models of computation, whereas computational systems operate as finite physical implementations whose behaviour is shaped by their architecture and physical substrate. In such systems, computational organisation cannot be directly characterised from algorithms or internal representations. Building on the use of reaction time distributions to constrain mechanisms in cognitive science, we introduce an empirical framework for investigating computation in physical systems by inferring computational mechanisms from observable computation time distributions. The framework combines empirical benchmarking, which characterises computational efficiency through the scaling of computation time with problem size, with distributional analysis, which treats computation time distributions as signatures of the stochastic dynamics generated by different computational mechanisms. We demonstrate the framework using a scalable visual search paradigm, comparing human performance with three computational models that employ distinct mechanisms of evidence accumulation. Benchmarking reveals broadly similar scaling behaviour for the models and human observers, whereas distributional analysis distinguishes between these models. We show that computation time distributions provide an empirical level of description linking observable computational behaviour to underlying computational mechanisms. This framework complements computational complexity theory by extending the empirical study of computation from measuring computational performance to characterising computational organisation. It provides an empirical approach for comparing finite computational systems across diverse physical implementations, including biological, classical, neuromorphic, and quantum systems, without requiring access to their internal algorithms or architectures.

Bayesian Geophysical Inversion using Stein Mixture Variational Inference for Multimodal or Complex Solutions
Authors: Xuebin Zhao, Andrew Curtis
First: 2026-10-16T15:19:26+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T15:19:26+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Bayesian geophysical inversion provides a probabilistic framework to estimate subsurface properties, in which uncertainties are represented by a so-called posterior probability density function (pdf). Estimating this pdf is computationally challenging when it is high dimensional or complex, particularly if it may be multimodal. We introduce Stein mixture variational inference (SMVI) for efficient Bayesian geophysical inversion with multimodal or complex posterior solutions. The method combines parametric variational inference with Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD). In SMVI, the posterior pdf is approximated by a mixture (sum) of distributions, in which component distributions are optimised jointly using the SVGD update rule. This construction combines computational efficiency with the flexibility to represent a range of plausible Earth models and allows components to be updated in parallel. Here, SMVI is first tested on two synthetic seismic inverse problems, demonstrating that SMVI can recover non-Gaussian, complex and multimodal posterior solutions accurately and efficiently. Then, we apply SMVI to real seismic data by performing Love wave tomography of the British Isles, and obtain inversion results that are consistent with previous Monte Carlo solutions. Around the East Irish Sea sedimentary basins, SMVI captures bimodal uncertainty associated with ambiguity in basin boundary locations, revealing alternative subsurface structures compatible with the observations. These results indicate that SMVI generalises various previous variational methods, and provides a practical and effective framework for quantifying complex uncertainties and supporting geological interpretation of seismic images.

Token-Level Attribution of Multi-Mode LLM Watermarks: Optimal Rule and Belief Weighting
Authors: Rui Zhou, Licheng Zhao, Chao Shen, Wenqiang Pu
First: 2026-10-16T15:18:28+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T15:18:28+00:00
Comments: 13 pages, 9 figures

Abstract
A watermark verifier may hold many modes, i.e., secret keys, and a document may mix spans of different modes with unwatermarked text. Directly reporting the mode with the largest pivotal statistic (pivot) inflates the Type I error, and restoring the nominal level reduces power as candidate modes are added. We formulate token-level multi-mode attribution for the Gumbel-max watermark as maximizing correct-label power under a Type I error constraint given a mode prior. We show that the optimal rule reports the mode with the largest prior-weighted alternative density and rejects when this density exceeds one common threshold. Based on this rule, we propose the Belief-weighted Likelihood Attribution with Density Estimation (BLADE) detector. It estimates the mode prior from neighboring pivots and the alternative density from reference token probabilities. Under a conditional product model of the pivots, it keeps the exact Type I error. We further show that BLADE approaches the optimal rule as its prior and density estimates improve, with a sharp coefficient on the density error. On OPT-1.3B and other open-weight large language models, BLADE attains higher correct-label power than the compared detectors across candidate counts, token-substitution rates, generation temperatures and generator models.

Weighted spatial averages with missing observations: Convergence and accuracy of delta-method variance approximations
Authors: Ashwin K Seshadri, Abhishek Pal Majumder
First: 2026-10-16T14:14:21+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T14:14:21+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Weighted spatial averages from incomplete networks are ratios whose variance is approximated by truncating a Taylor series (the delta method). Three questions remain open: whether the series converges, how large a truncation's error is, and how it behaves as the network grows. We address these for observations missing completely at random, independently with a common probability $α$. First, the truncations, which count corrections for the denominator (the total reported weight) and for its coupling with the numerator, converge for a given network only if $α>\tfrac12$ and both corrections increase together. Second, whether or not the series converges, the error of truncations correcting only for the denominator is controlled at any network size by its coefficient of variation $ρ$, with $ρ^2=\tfrac{1-α}α\sum_iβ_i^2$ for weights $β_i$, and by the probability of rare reporting patterns. Third, for uniform weights and bounded fields, these errors vanish as the network grows, at any fixed availability. If correlations also decay with distance, the first-order approximation of earlier work has error of order $ρ^4$, against $ρ^2$ for the simplest approximation. Only truncations of equal order in numerator and denominator, beginning with the first-order truncation, avoid spurious dependence on the mean level; higher ones need not be more accurate. The exact variance for uniform weights, a single sum, shows that for correlated fields the first-order approximation overstates the contribution of missing data but is conservative overall. Concentrated weights degrade it more than $ρ$ indicates. Rainfall simulations over India illustrates results, several of which extend to self-normalized estimators.

Abstract
Change points that arrive in quick succession, or near the boundaries of the sample, are prone to type-II error: the few observations trapped between successive breaks carry too little information for global procedures to analyze. This article develops a test statistic, $I_1$, for which we derive the limiting null distribution under Gaussian noise, extend the theory to weakly dependent sequences, and prove consistency under a mean-shift alternative. An application to the US-Iran 2026 conflict quantifies the exposure of energy markets to geopolitical shocks using two new metrics, event sensitivity and event attribution, leading to time series tokenization.

Abstract
As climate change intensifies, floods are becoming more frequent and destructive, making accurate flood understanding an urgent priority. However, existing vision-language models perform poorly on flood-related tasks, suffering from attentional bias and inaccurate descriptions of flood magnitude. To bridge this gap, we construct the first large-scale remote sensing flood multi-modal benchmark that supports language-level flood understanding and pixel-level flood segmentation. Abstract
We develop a semiparametric kinematic state-space framework for estimating the latent dynamics of global warming from noisy monthly temperature observations. The framework estimates the warming level, warming rate, and warming acceleration jointly, while incorporating covariates, measurement error, serial dependence, and time-varying error variance directly in the model. Applying the framework to five major global temperature records, and comparing it with nonparametric kernel and parametric benchmark estimators, we show that the inferred recent warming dynamics depend crucially on the parametric restrictions imposed on the warming path. The current underlying warming level is robustly estimated across datasets and methods, with estimates close to \(1.40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) above pre-industrial levels. Estimates of the current warming rate differ materially across estimator classes: flexible local estimators imply substantially higher current rates than the parametric benchmarks. Our preferred state-space specification estimates the current warming rate at approximately \(0.47\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C} \, \textnormal{decade}^{-1}\). Endpoint acceleration estimates from the flexible estimators are positive across all five records, although their statistical significance varies across records and methods. Average acceleration since 2015 is significantly positive across all five records in the preferred state-space model, with a cross-dataset mean of approximately \(0.19\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C} \, \textnormal{decade}^{-2}\). Our results indicate that global warming is proceeding faster, and has accelerated more strongly in the recent period, than conventional estimates suggest.

First: 2025-04-28T09:19:01+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T11:58:42+00:00

Abstract
In rugby union and association football, team key performance indicators (KPIs) can be represented in absolute terms or relative to the opponent. Relativisation sometimes improves match-outcome prediction and sometimes harms it. There has been no general account of when each occurs. The answer depends on how much the two teams differ in variability and how strongly their KPI values rise and fall together. We combine these properties into the Paired Efficiency Factor (PEF), which generalises Fisher's paired-efficiency result to the unequal-variance conditions typical of competitive sport. The PEF also connects a KPI's statistical efficiency to how much information its relative form carries about the outcome. Combined KPIs can interact in complex ways, so we analyse each indicator on its own. Across 86 team KPIs from professional rugby union and association football, the PEF places every metric in one of four regimes. Anti-correlation is common among these KPIs, especially for high-volume competitive counts, and absolute measures are then usually preferable. Relativisation can still improve prediction when a noisier difference carries more outcome information. An idealised simulation and one representative KPI from each regime confirm both signs under team-blocked cross-validation. The same pairing geometry appears in healthcare, genomics, finance, and manufacturing. The PEF is a diagnostic for when to relativise a performance metric and when not to.

Abstract
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) have gained attention for solving partial differential equations, including the scattered Helmholtz equation, due to their flexibility and mesh-free formulation. However, their performance suffers from low-frequency bias, particularly in high-frequency wavefield simulations, limiting convergence speed and accuracy. To address this, we propose a novel and simplified PINN framework that incorporates explicit, trainable Gabor basis functions to efficiently capture the localized and oscillatory nature of wavefields. Unlike previous Gabor-based PINNs that rely on multiplicative filters or auxiliary networks to learn Gabor parameters, our approach redefines the network's task as learning a nonlinear mapping from input coordinates to a custom Gabor coordinate system, where a Gabor function captures the dominant oscillatory behavior of the wavefield. This formulation absorbs the effect of two Gabor parameters into the learned mapping, reducing computational complexity and eliminating the need for manual tuning of hyperparameters. Numerical experiments on various velocity models show that our Gabor-PINN achieves faster convergence, higher accuracy, and greater robustness to architectural design and initialization compared to both traditional PINNs and prior Gabor-based methods. The improvement lies not in adding architectural complexity --as is common in enhanced PINNs -- but in absorbing this complexity into the learned coordinate transformation, making the method both simpler and more effective. Our implementation is publicly available to support reproducibility and future research.

Abstract
Understanding how much and where information is nested in sets of data obtained using different parameters and metrics is a typical problem in the analysis of high-dimensional datasets. Approaches based on relative data-driven metrics may be poorly quantitative and difficult to interpret, while accurate methods may be computationally expensive and impractical for large datasets. Here, we report an efficient, quantitative, and transferable method useful to this end, which we call "Micro-Information Analysis". Its foundation is based on the tight relationship that exists between the microscopic features of data distributions and the information they contain. The method performs preliminary unsupervised clustering, capturing microstructural features of data distributions such as the dominant Gaussian microdomains (microstates) present in them, their population, separation, and their residual non-Gaussian fraction. We prove the efficiency of our method by analyzing and ranking data generated synthetically or from molecular simulations, demonstrating how "Micro-Information Analysis" is a physically-grounded quantitative tool useful for the study of physical systems from their data and for data science in general.

Abstract
From neuroscience and genomics to systems biology and ecology, researchers rely on clustering similarity data to uncover modular structure. Most methods work in two steps: first reduce noise, then cluster. The first step runs without regard to the second: it forces arbitrary choices, discards useful information, and can impose structure where none exists. Here we bring the two steps together. Using established Bayesian community detection methods, we let one minimum description length criterion choose both how much to sparsify the data and how to cluster it. This one-step approach keeps all the samples for inference, removes the arbitrary threshold choice, and returns a single cluster when there is no real structure. On synthetic data, our approach recovers clusters under more noise and with fewer samples than standard methods. On gene co-expression data from the human brain, it finds more coherent and more stable clusters than WGCNA, the standard tool in the field. Uniting sparsification and clustering in a single model-selection step provides a principled, noise-resistant way to uncover modular structure in similarity data across fields.

Abstract
We compute exactly the covariance between the maximum $M$ and the number of records $K$ in a discrete time series of $n$ entries as a quantitative probe of the history leading to an extreme event. For continuous IID series, with a common probability distribution function (PDF) $p(x)$, we find the striking result that $M$ and $K$ are uncorrelated for any $n \geq 1$. Abstract

Abstract
With only one confirmed example, namely the Earth, the abundance of life across the universe remains deeply uncertain, and local evidence about Earth's particular circumstances can significantly shape our knowledge of life elsewhere.
