Abstract
The generalized persistence (GP) model, developed in the context of estimating ``value added'' by individual teachers to their students' current and future test scores, is one of the most flexible value-added models in the literature. Although developed in the educational setting, the GP model can potentially be applied to any structure where each sequential response of a lower-level unit may be associated with a different higher-level unit, and the effects of the higher-level units may persist over time. The flexibility of the GP model, however, and its multiple membership random effects structure lead to computational challenges that have limited the model's availability. We develop an EM algorithm to compute maximum likelihood estimates efficiently for the GP model, making use of the sparse structure of the random effects and error covariance matrices. The algorithm is implemented in the package GPvam in R statistical software. We give examples of the computations and illustrate the gains in computational efficiency achieved by our estimation procedure.

Abstract
The in-Situ Thunderstorm Observer for Radiation Mechanisms (iSTORM) is a gamma-ray spectrometer to study gamma-ray transients originating from thunderstorms, such as glows and terrestrial gamma-ray flashes (TGFs). It is designed and built by the U.S. Naval Research Laboratory for deployment aboard a NASA ER-2 aircraft. Using an array of 32 one-inch-diameter $\mathrm{CeBr}_3$ scintillators read out with silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs), the instrument achieves an energy range of $\sim 250 \ \mathrm{keV}$ to $5 \ \mathrm{MeV}$ under flight conditions, with a total geometrical area of $157 \ \mathrm{cm^2}$. One of two gamma-ray instruments in the ALOFT campaign, iSTORM recorded glows, terrestrial gamma-ray flashes (TGFs), and the newly discovered flickering gamma-ray flashes (FGFs).

When does boundary-condition uncertainty outweigh thermal and vegetation effects in street canyon flow? A global sensitivity analysis of input uncertainty
Authors: Albert Moreno Català, Joan Calafell Sandiumenge, Nikolaj T. Mücke, Jan Mateu Armengol
First: 2026-10-23T13:43:27+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T13:43:27+00:00

Abstract
Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) is increasingly used to resolve wind, heat, and pollution patterns in cities with high fidelity but high computational cost. Thermal effects and vegetation are often neglected despite being important drivers, yet their influence may be masked by variability from other uncertain inputs. This work determines how precisely model parameters and boundary conditions must be known for these effects to govern output uncertainty in the velocity, temperature, and concentration fields of a two-dimensional street canyon. A global Sobol analysis of 24 parameters is made tractable by a surrogate trained on Reynolds-Averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) simulations, combining principal component analysis (PCA) with multilayer perceptrons (MLPs). Two ventilation regimes assess the variance due to thermal and vegetation parameters as uncertainty in all other inputs is progressively reduced. At typical uncertainty levels, turbulence closure constants and inflow turbulence profiles govern variability in all three fields in both regimes. Under weak ventilation, solar forcing becomes the main source of temperature variability once the remaining uncertainties are halved, and of concentration variance once they are reduced to a quarter (about $\pm11\%$ for the dominant closure constant and $\pm12\%$ for the emission rate). In contrast, vegetation contributes significantly at typical uncertainty levels. Under strong ventilation, however, the canopy never overtakes the closure constants, even when the dominant one is known to $\pm5\%$. Spatially, both effects peak at road level and along canyon walls, which influence outdoor exposure and indoor infiltration, suggesting that high-resolution exposure studies should account for them.

Abstract
Statistical post-processing can calibrate the individual components of an ensemble forecast but may also distort relationships between forecast dimensions. This paper proposes Score-Optimized Copula Coupling, which rearranges calibrated forecast samples according to an optimized dependence structure to restore potentially lost dependencies. We assess fixed and adaptively updated versions using wind-speed and temperature forecasts. In the variable-specific spatial case studies, all versions significantly outperform the strongest benchmarks, with improvements of 1.08-2.02 percent in energy score and 5.45-7.97 percent in variogram score. A third case study at 24 co-located stations shows that one optimized structure can represent both spatial and inter-variable relationships. The largest energy and variogram score improvements are 3.98 and 3.69 percent for spatial dependence and 1.16 and 1.06 percent for inter-variable dependence; all are statistically significant. Additional calibration terms improve covariance representation but can reduce gains in the other scores.

Derivation of the Sample-Size Scaling of TWO-NN Intrinsic-Dimension Estimates from Molecular Dynamics Trajectories

Comments: 11 pages, 4 figures; Supplementary Material included
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
The intrinsic dimension of a dataset is the number of independent directions needed to describe the space occupied by its data. Nearest-neighbor estimators infer it from how the probability of finding a neighbor grows around each sampled point. Because the neighbor distances $r$ decrease as the sample grows, the estimated dimension can depend strongly on the number $N$ of available points, which in molecular dynamics is set by trajectory length and saving frequency. We derive the large-sample behavior of the TWO-NN estimator for data drawn from a smooth $d$-dimensional space. The typical nearest-neighbor distance scales as $N^{-1/d}$, and smooth deviations from local uniformity produce corrections in powers of $N^{-2/d}$. Isotropic noise adds a correction proportional to $N^{+2/d}$; for a five-dimensional hyperplane in 100 noisy features, the extended expansion recovers the dimension within a few percent when noise is moderate. We test the scaling on ten independent 100 $μ$s simulations of alanine dipeptide, represented by the pairwise distances among its ten heavy atoms, whose geometric dimension is $3n_{\mathrm{at}}-6=24$. Over the investigated range, the estimate does not depend systematically on the temporal spacing between configurations, but increases from $7.6$ to $15.6$ as $N$ grows from $10^2$ to $2\times10^5$. Extrapolations that retain corrections through $r^2$, $r^4$, and $r^6$ give limiting dimensions of $24.81$, $23.47$, and $25.30$, respectively. All three estimates lie within 6% of the known dimension and bracket it, and their spread measures the uncertainty due to truncation. The derived scaling provides a practical route from finite-sample estimates to the geometric dimension.

The intrinsic dimension of a dataset is the number of independent directions needed to describe the space occupied by its data.

The Geometry of Existence and Uniqueness of Maximum Likelihood Estimation in Categorical Response Models
Authors: Lukas Sablica, Kurt Hornik, Thomas Rusch
First: 2026-10-06T10:29:07+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T11:35:00+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Nonexistence of the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) under separation is treated as a solved problem for binary logistic regression and as a scattered collection of model-specific results everywhere else. We show that it is one phenomenon with one criterion. In a latent polyhedral categorical response model, every observed outcome corresponds to a polyhedral event in latent variables whose faces shift linearly with the parameter. Random-utility choice, cumulative-link, ranking, multivariate binary and ordinal, sequential, adjacent-category logit, and fixed-score stereotype models belong to this family. The likelihood sees each observed factor only through the columns of its threshold map, the structure vectors. A finite MLE exists if and only if the pooled structure vector set has overlap. Sufficiency requires only continuity, necessity requires only strictly threshold-increasing probabilities, and neither likelihood concavity, exchangeability, nor full design rank is needed. Positive strictly log-concave latent densities and threshold-identifiable polyhedra then yield uniqueness on the estimable span. A single linear program determines whether overlap holds, and convex cone geometry measures the dimension of separation. An application using cumulative-link models for willingness to share health data identifies observations and model terms associated with nonexistence and illustrates how the diagnostics inform model revision and sensitivity analysis.

Direct Simulation of Poisson and Newton Kernel Distributions
Authors: Lukas Sablica, Kurt Hornik
First: 2026-10-23T11:28:16+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T11:28:16+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
The Poisson kernel-based (PKB) distribution is a flexible and numerically convenient family on the unit sphere used for model-based clustering, uniformity testing, and directional data analysis. Available random variate generators rely on rejection from a von Mises-Fisher, angular central Gaussian, or projected Saw envelope and therefore require a rejection loop, tuning or root-finding, or a univariate setup phase. We show that none is needed. A uniformly random line through the interior point $ρμ$ meets the sphere in two points; selecting one with probability proportional to the opposite segment length returns exactly the target density. The sampler is exact, rejection- and tuning-free, uses one uniform direction and one Bernoulli draw, and costs $O(d)$ operations. We place the distribution in the power-Saw family of densities proportional to $\|x-ρμ\|^{-ξ}$ and show that, for $d\geq3$, the same construction is exact for one further exponent. This introduces the Newton kernel-based (NKB) distribution, whose normalizing constant is identically one. Across a wide grid of dimensions, concentrations, and sample sizes, the direct sampler is faster than the better of two previously recommended methods in every case, with the largest gains at moderate concentration.

Abstract
Synthetic controls are used for one treated unit that lies outside its donors' range, with a pre-treatment window too short for residual-based standard errors to be trusted. We propose inference that separates the periods used to fit the synthetic control from the periods used to assess its error. Weights are fitted on an early block of pre-treatment periods, and the gap on a later block calibrates the bias and the noise. The resulting interval has finite-sample coverage under an explicit restriction on how far the counterfactual may depart from the extrapolated pre-treatment gap, for any fitting rule that uses only the early block. The leading restriction bounds the curvature of the gap and leaves its slope free, and the breakdown curvature at which the interval first includes zero states how much extrapolation a conclusion needs. We show when drift bounds calibrated from donor placebos are valid and how to allow for serial dependence. In simulations, conventional intervals undercover when the treated unit drifts from its donors, while the proposed interval covers whenever its restriction holds and the noise is serially independent. Re-analyses of three classic applications show that some well-known conclusions rest on far stronger extrapolation than others.

Abstract
Dual active galactic nuclei (DAGN) mark a critical phase in the evolution of merging galaxies and the pairing of supermassive black holes, yet they remain difficult to identify in large imaging surveys because of projection effects and limited spatial resolution. Compact foreground stars and unresolved substructure can mimic dual nuclei through chance superposition, complicating automated detection. We revisit the 46,061 galaxies flagged but rejected as DAGN candidates by the GOTHIC pipeline, primarily because the two nuclei fell within the SDSS fibre aperture or exceeded its separation threshold. Spectroscopy of the most compact ($\le 1$ kpc) systems shows spectra dominated by stellar absorption, with no resolved double-peaked emission, so confirmation requires higher-resolution follow-up. The catalogue is a statistically refined list of candidates, not confirmed DAGN. Nonetheless, deep-learning detection substantially reduces contamination and expands the plausible DAGN census.

Abstract
Meta-analysis aims to synthesize multiple studies to obtain a summary estimate of a population parameter of interest. However, existing meta-analysis methods require strong assumptions that render them not robust to bias and heterogeneity of the studies. In particular, fixed-effect meta-analysis assumes a common parameter across the studies, whereas random-effects meta-analysis models heterogeneity as independent and identically distributed random effects, which still implies a common parameter marginally over the random effects. Due to their limitations, we propose a bias-aware meta-analysis framework that allows for heterogeneous studies with fixed, unknown biases. With no bias, our bias-aware meta-analysis reduces to fixed-effect meta-analysis; with a given bound on the bias, we can still use the fixed-effect meta-analysis point estimator and standard error, but we must inflate the critical value for confidence intervals from the standard normal quantile to a folded normal quantile. We recommend reporting the maximum bias for significance, the maximum value of the bias such that the confidence intervals exclude the null value. We propose a formal model checking procedure and recommend reporting the minimum bias for compatibility, the minimum value of the bias such that the bias-aware meta-analysis model is compatible with the observed data. We discuss the practical implementation of our method and, in particular, the approaches to calibrating the maximum bias for significance. We also compare our method with traditional fixed-effect and random-effects meta-analyses.

Abstract

Abstract
Background-error covariance models are central to atmospheric data assimilation because they spread partial observational information across space, variables, and dynamically coupled scales. Ensemble and variational methods exploit this covariance geometry efficiently, but their standard analysis formulations are largely built around Gaussian or locally quadratic approximations. Abstract
Geometric process (GP) models are widely used for repairable systems whose inter-failure times evolve over successive failure-repair cycles. Abstract
Kolmogorov--Arnold Networks (KANs) offer a promising path toward interpretable machine learning: their learnable activations can be studied individually while collectively fitting complex data accurately. Abstract
Background: Proportions are common in interrupted time series analysis (ITSA), but bounded outcomes and serial dependence require distinct modeling considerations. Abstract
When a micron-sized bubble bursts, capillary waves deform the cavity into a cone that ejects a Worthington jet. Abstract
Calibration of mean estimates is a crucial property in many applications, particularly in actuarial decision-making. Tests of mean-calibration are often performed under the assumption that the estimated means correctly preserve the ordering of the corresponding population means. In this paper, we first consider the classical Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT) within the Exponential Dispersion Family (EDF), and we show that applying likelihood-based methods to test mean-calibration within the EDF requires a strong ordering assumption on the mean estimates. This assumption makes the null hypothesis of the mean-calibration test equivalent to the correct specification of the mean estimates. Next, we investigate the framework of universal inference for testing mean-calibration and mean-specification, and the relationship between the tests. We develop a sub-sampled split LRT within the EDF that provides finite-sample guarantees with universally valid critical values. We investigate type I error, power, and e-power of the sub-sampled split LRT and compare its performance with that of the classical LRT. We also propose a novel test statistic based on a sub-sampled split $L_q$-likelihood ratio test (L$q$RT) to enhance the performance of the statistical test. Numerical results demonstrate that universal inference based on the sub-sampled split LRT and sub-sampled split L$q$RT provides an attractive alternative to the classical LRT within the EDF, achieving high power for detecting miscalibration and misspecification in medium- and large-sized samples.
